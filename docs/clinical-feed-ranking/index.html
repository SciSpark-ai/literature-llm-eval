Study 2 · ranking

LLM or cross-encoder: which ranks a clinical literature feed better?

Comparing an LLM and a cross-encoder for ranking a clinical literature feed

  • 3 topics
  • 682 papers
  • GPT-4o-mini vs Jina reranker v2
  • human relevance labels
  • replay of saved scores

Background

A literature recommender shows its reader a short list, so the order of the first few papers matters most. In SciSpark Psychiatry, an LLM scores each paper's relevance from 0 to 4 using a clinical rubric. An LLM can apply such a rubric, but it costs one call per paper, and five possible scores leave many papers tied. A cross-encoder, a small ranking model that reads the query and the paper together, is fast and gives every paper a distinct score, but it knows no rubric.

How do the two compare for ranking a clinical feed, and can they be combined?

Approach

We compared the two on 682 papers from the same three CLEF 2019 Technology-Assisted Review topics as Study 1: every relevant paper by the reviews' abstract screening decisions (16, 37 and 29 per topic) plus 200 irrelevant papers per topic, chosen by a fixed hash. The LLM was GPT-4o-mini at temperature zero with the production clinical rubric, one paper per request; the cross-encoder was the Jina reranker v2 (base, multilingual). Both saw the same title and the first 1,800 characters of each abstract. Because the LLM received a structured clinical topic and the cross-encoder a one-sentence query, we compare two working configurations rather than two model architectures.

We measured ranking with two metrics. nDCG@10 asks whether relevant papers sit at the very top of the list (from 0 to 1; 1 is ideal). Recall@50 is the share of all relevant papers that appear in the first 50 results. Where scores tie, both metrics are averaged over random orders within the tie.

Results

The LLM ranked relevant papers higher, but coverage depended on the topic

The LLM had a higher nDCG@10 on all three topics and a higher Recall@50 on two. On depression with COPD, however, the cross-encoder placed all 16 relevant papers in its first 50 results while the LLM placed 78.8%, so the two metrics pointed in opposite directions. The results also depended on the label used: measured against final inclusion after full-text review rather than abstract screening, the LLM's nDCG@10 on treatment-resistant depression fell from 0.628 to 0.209.

A cross-encoder helped to order papers within an LLM score

The LLM gave its top score to 43 papers on treatment-resistant depression and 37 on insomnia, too many to order by score alone. Ordering papers by LLM score and then by cross-encoder score raised nDCG@10 on two topics and lowered it on one. Such a tie-break cannot rescue a paper that the LLM scored too low.

Cutting the pool with the cross-encoder saved calls but lost relevant papers

If only the cross-encoder's top 50 or top 100 papers were sent to the LLM, the number of LLM decisions would fall from 682 to 150 or 300. A paper cut at this step, however, never reaches the LLM. On treatment-resistant depression, the top-50 cut left nDCG@10 unchanged at 0.783 while Recall@50 fell from 81.1% to 59.5%; a top-of-list metric alone would have missed this loss.

Implications for LLM literature recommenders

Together, these results suggest that LLM scores are a strong basis for ordering the top of a clinical feed, and that their main weakness, coarse ties, can be addressed with a cheaper fine-grained score. They also show that any step placed before the LLM limits what the LLM can recover. We therefore suggest using the LLM score for the top of the feed and breaking its ties with a cheaper score, checked topic by topic. A candidate pool should not be shrunk before the LLM without measuring recall: in our data the top 100 kept 86–100% of relevant papers, against 59–100% for the top 50. Finally, a top-of-list metric and a coverage metric should be reported together, because each revealed a problem the other missed.

Limitations

All three topics were used during development, and each paper was scored once, so this study does not measure run-to-run variation (see Study 1). Irrelevant papers were sampled, so relevant papers are far more common than in a real search. Screening labels are not a clinician's judgment of usefulness. The combined designs replay saved scores, so no live latency or cost was measured.
